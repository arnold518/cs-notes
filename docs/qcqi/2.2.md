# § 2.2. The Postulates of Quantum Mechanics

## 2.2.1 State space

!!! definition "Definition 2.2.1 : Postulate 1 : State space"
    A **state space** of an isolated physical system is a complex vector space with inner product, that is, a Hilbert space.
    The system is completely described by a **state vector**, which is a unit vector in the system's state space.

!!! concept "Concept 2.2.2 : Qubit state and normalization"
    The simplest quantum mechanical system, and the one we will be most concerned with, is the **qubit**.
    A qubit has a two-dimensional state space.
    Suppose $|0\rangle$ and $|1\rangle$ form an orthonormal basis.

    $$
    \begin{equation*}
    |\psi\rangle = a|0\rangle+b|1\rangle
    \end{equation*}
    $$

    where $a$ and $b$ are complex numbers.
    
    The condition that $|\psi\rangle$ be a unit vector is $\langle\psi \mid \psi\rangle=1$.
    This is equivalent to $|a|^{2}+|b|^{2}=1$.
    The condition $\langle\psi \mid \psi\rangle=1$ is the **normalization condition** for state vectors.

!!! concept "Concept 2.2.3 : Superpositions, amplitude for state"
    The way a qubit differs from a bit is that superpositions of the form $a|0\rangle+b|1\rangle$ can exist.
    In such a state, it is not possible to say that the qubit is definitely in state $|0\rangle$ or definitely in state $|1\rangle$.
    For any linear combination $\sum_{i} \alpha_{i}\left|\psi_{i}\right\rangle$, we call it a **superposition** of the states $\left|\psi_{i}\right\rangle$ with **amplitude** $\alpha_{i}$ for state $\left|\psi_{i}\right\rangle$.

    In particular,

    $$
    \frac{|0\rangle-|1\rangle}{\sqrt{2}}
    $$

    this is a superposition of the states $|0\rangle$ and $|1\rangle$ with amplitude $1 / \sqrt{2}$ for state $|0\rangle$, and amplitude $-1 / \sqrt{2}$ for state $|1\rangle$.

## 2.2.2. Evolution

!!! definition "Definition 2.2.4 : Postulate 2 : Unitary time evolution"
    For a closed quantum system, the **evolution** is given by a **unitary transformation**.
    If $|\psi\rangle$ is the state at time $t_{1}$ and $|\psi^{\prime}\rangle$ is the state at time $t_{2}$, then the evolution is related by a unitary operator $U$ that depends only on $t_{1}$ and $t_{2}$ such that

    $$
    |\psi^{\prime}\rangle = U|\psi\rangle.
    $$

!!! concept "Concept 2.2.5 : Hadamard gate"
    The Hadamard gate is a unitary operator denoted by $H$.
    It acts on basis states as
    
    $$
    H|0\rangle \equiv \frac{|0\rangle+|1\rangle}{\sqrt{2}}, H|1\rangle \equiv \frac{|0\rangle-|1\rangle}{\sqrt{2}}.
    $$

    Its matrix representation is

    $$
    H=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left[\begin{array}{cc}
    1 & 1 \\
    1 & -1
    \end{array}\right]
    $$

!!! theorem "Theorem 2.2.6 : Properties of the Hadamard gate"
    1. The Hadamard gate is unitary, i.e.

        $$
        H^{\dagger} H=I, \quad H H^{\dagger}=I.
        $$

    2. The square of the Hadamard gate is the identity,

        $$
        H^{2}=I.
        $$

    3. The eigenvalues of $H$ are $+1$ and $-1$, with corresponding normalized eigenvectors

        $$
        |\lambda_{+}\rangle=\cos\frac{\pi}{8}|0\rangle+\sin\frac{\pi}{8}|1\rangle,\quad
        H|\lambda_{+}\rangle=|\lambda_{+}\rangle,
        $$

        $$
        |\lambda_{-}\rangle=\sin\frac{\pi}{8}|0\rangle-\cos\frac{\pi}{8}|1\rangle,\quad
        H|\lambda_{-}\rangle=-|\lambda_{-}\rangle.
        $$

    !!! proof
        Using the matrix representation

        $$
        H=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}
        \begin{pmatrix}
        1 & 1 \\
        1 & -1
        \end{pmatrix},
        $$

        we have $H^{\dagger}=H$.
        Therefore

        $$
        H^{\dagger}H
        =H^{2}
        =\frac{1}{2}
        \begin{pmatrix}
        1 & 1 \\
        1 & -1
        \end{pmatrix}^{2}
        =\begin{pmatrix}
        1 & 0 \\
        0 & 1
        \end{pmatrix}=I.
        $$

        So $H$ is unitary and $H^{2}=I$.
        If $H|\psi\rangle=\lambda|\psi\rangle$ for a nonzero vector $|\psi\rangle$, then $H^{2}|\psi\rangle=\lambda^{2}|\psi\rangle=|\psi\rangle$, so $\lambda=\pm 1$.
        Direct substitution gives

        $$
        H|\lambda_{+}\rangle
        =\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}
        \begin{pmatrix}
        \cos\frac{\pi}{8}+\sin\frac{\pi}{8} \\
        \cos\frac{\pi}{8}-\sin\frac{\pi}{8}
        \end{pmatrix}
        =
        \begin{pmatrix}
        \cos\frac{\pi}{8} \\
        \sin\frac{\pi}{8}
        \end{pmatrix}
        =|\lambda_{+}\rangle
        $$

        and

        $$
        H|\lambda_{-}\rangle
        =\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}
        \begin{pmatrix}
        \sin\frac{\pi}{8}-\cos\frac{\pi}{8} \\
        \sin\frac{\pi}{8}+\cos\frac{\pi}{8}
        \end{pmatrix}
        =
        -\begin{pmatrix}
        \sin\frac{\pi}{8} \\
        -\cos\frac{\pi}{8}
        \end{pmatrix}
        =-|\lambda_{-}\rangle.
        $$

!!! definition "Definition 2.2.7 : Postulate 2' : Schrödinger equation"
    The time evolution of the state of a closed quantum system is given by the **Schrödinger equation**.

    $$
    i\hbar\frac{d|\psi\rangle}{dt}=H|\psi\rangle.
    $$

    In this equation, $\hbar$ is a physical constant known as Planck's constant whose value must be experimentally determined.
    The exact value is not important to us.
    In practice, it is common to absorb the factor $\hbar$ into $H$, effectively setting $\hbar=1$.
    $H$ is a fixed **Hermitian operator** known as the **Hamiltonian** of the closed system.

!!! concept "Concept 2.2.8 : Spectral decomposition of the Hamiltonian"
    The Hamiltonian is Hermitian, so it has the following spectral decomposition.

    $$
    H = \sum_{E} E|E\rangle\langle E|.
    $$

    The vectors $|E\rangle$ are the **energy eigenstates**, sometimes called **stationary states**, and the corresponding eigenvalue $E$ is the **energy** of that state.
    The lowest energy is the **ground state energy**, and its corresponding eigenspace is the **ground state**.
    
    These states are stationary because their only time dependence is an overall phase factor.

    $$
    |E\rangle \rightarrow \exp(-iEt/\hbar)|E\rangle.
    $$

!!! example "Example 2.2.9 : Hamiltonian example"
    As an example, suppose a single qubit has Hamiltonian

    $$
    H=\hbar \omega X
    $$

    In this equation $\omega$ is a parameter that, in practice, needs to be experimentally determined.
    We won't worry about the parameter overly much here - the point is to give you a feel for the sort of Hamiltonians that are sometimes written down in the study of quantum computation and quantum information.
    
    The energy eigenstates of this Hamiltonian are obviously the same as the eigenstates of $X$, namely $(|0\rangle+|1\rangle) / \sqrt{2}$ and $(|0\rangle-|1\rangle) / \sqrt{2}$, with corresponding energies $\hbar \omega$ and $-\hbar \omega$.
    The ground state is therefore $(|0\rangle-|1\rangle) / \sqrt{2}$, and the ground state energy is $-\hbar \omega$.

!!! theorem "Lemma 2.2.10"
    Suppose $A$, $B$ and $H$ are Hermitian operators and let

    $$
    U\left(t_{1}, t_{2}\right) \equiv \exp \left[\frac{-i H\left(t_{2}-t_{1}\right)}{\hbar}\right].
    $$

    - (a) If $[A,B]=0$, then $\exp(A)\exp(B)=\exp(A+B)$.
    - (b) $U(t_1,t_2)$ is unitary.
    - (c) For any unitary $U$, the operator $K\equiv -i\log(U)$ is Hermitian and $U=\exp(iK)$.

    !!! proof
        - (a)
            Expand both exponentials into power series.

            $$
            \exp(A)\exp(B)=\left(\sum_{m=0}^{\infty} \frac{A^{m}}{m!}\right)\left(\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{B^{n}}{n!}\right)
            =\sum_{m,n\ge 0} \frac{A^{m}B^{n}}{m!n!}.
            $$

            Since $A$ and $B$ commute, the binomial expansion is

            $$
            (A+B)^r=\sum_{m=0}^{r}\binom{r}{m}A^mB^{r-m}.
            $$

            Therefore

            $$
            \exp(A)\exp(B)
            =\sum_{r=0}^{\infty}\sum_{m=0}^{r} \frac{\binom{r}{m} A^{m}B^{r-m}}{r!}
            =\sum_{r=0}^{\infty}\frac{(A+B)^r}{r!}
            =\exp(A+B).
            $$

        - (b)
            By definition,
        
            $$
            U(t_1,t_2)=\exp\left[-\frac{iH(t_2-t_1)}{\hbar}\right].
            $$
        
            Since $H$ is Hermitian,
        
            $$
            U(t_1,t_2)^\dagger
            =
            \exp\left[\frac{iH(t_2-t_1)}{\hbar}\right].
            $$
        
            The two exponents commute because both are scalar multiples of $H$.
            Therefore, by part (a),
        
            $$
            U(t_1,t_2)^\dagger U(t_1,t_2)
            =
            \exp\left[\frac{iH(t_2-t_1)}{\hbar}\right]
            \exp\left[-\frac{iH(t_2-t_1)}{\hbar}\right]
            =
            \exp(0)
            =
            I.
            $$
        
            Similarly,
        
            $$
            U(t_1,t_2)U(t_1,t_2)^\dagger=I.
            $$
        
            Hence $U(t_1,t_2)$ is unitary.

        - (c)
            Since $U$ is unitary, it is normal, so it admits a spectral decomposition
        
            $$
            U=\sum_a e^{i\theta_a}|a\rangle\langle a|,
            $$
        
            where each $\theta_a\in\mathbb{R}$.
        
            Define
        
            $$
            K\equiv \sum_a \theta_a |a\rangle\langle a|.
            $$
        
            Then $K$ is Hermitian, since its eigenvalues are real:
        
            $$
            K^\dagger
            =
            \sum_a \theta_a |a\rangle\langle a|
            =
            K.
            $$
        
            Also,
        
            $$
            e^{iK}
            =
            \sum_a e^{i\theta_a}|a\rangle\langle a|
            =
            U.
            $$
        
            Thus every unitary operator can be written in the form $U=e^{iK}$ for some Hermitian operator $K$.
            With a chosen branch of the logarithm, one may write $K=-i\log(U)$.

!!! concept "Concept 2.2.11 : Relation between postulate 2 and postulate 2'"
    The connection between the Hamiltonian picture of dynamics, Postulate 2' (**Definition 2.2.7**), and the unitary operator picture, Postulate 2 (**Definition 2.2.4**), is obtained by solving Schrödinger's equation.
    The solution is

    $$
    \left|\psi\left(t_{2}\right)\right\rangle=\exp \left[\frac{-i H\left(t_{2}-t_{1}\right)}{\hbar}\right]\left|\psi\left(t_{1}\right)\right\rangle=U\left(t_{1}, t_{2}\right)\left|\psi\left(t_{1}\right)\right\rangle
    $$

    where

    $$
    U\left(t_{1}, t_{2}\right) \equiv \exp \left[\frac{-i H\left(t_{2}-t_{1}\right)}{\hbar}\right]
    $$

    This operator is unitary, and using **Lemma 2.2.10**, any unitary operator $U$ can be realized in the form $U=\exp(iK)$ for some Hermitian operator $K$.
    There is therefore a one-to-one correspondence between the discrete-time description of dynamics using unitary operators and the continuous-time description using Hamiltonians.
    For most of these notes, we use the unitary formulation of quantum dynamics.

!!! concept "Concept 2.2.12 : Approximation of an unclosed system to a time-varying hamiltonian"
    When we apply a unitary operator to a quantum system, we are often modeling an interaction with external controls rather than a perfectly closed system.
    For example, a laser interacting with an atom can be included in a larger closed atom-laser Hamiltonian, while the atom alone is described by a reduced effective Hamiltonian.
    This effective Hamiltonian contains laser-controlled parameters such as intensity and phase that experimenters can vary during an experiment.
    
    As a result, the atomic evolution is approximately unitary with respect to a Hamiltonian that varies in time.
    Thus, in many situations we work with time-varying Hamiltonians as a practical approximation for systems that are not strictly closed.
    The main non-unitary exception considered here is measurement, with more realistic open-system deviations discussed later.

## 2.2.3. Quantum Measurement

!!! definition "Definition 2.2.13 : Postulate 3 : Quantum measurements"
    Postulate 3 states that **quantum measurements** are described by a collection $\left\{M_{m}\right\}$ of **measurement operators**.
    These are operators acting on the state space of the system being measured.
    The index $m$ refers to the **measurement outcomes** that may occur in the experiment.
    If the state of the quantum system is $|\psi\rangle$ immediately before the measurement, then the probability that result $m$ occurs is given by

    $$
    p(m)=\langle\psi| M_{m}^{\dagger} M_{m}|\psi\rangle,
    $$

    and the state of the system after the measurement is

    $$
    \frac{M_{m}|\psi\rangle}{\sqrt{\langle\psi| M_{m}^{\dagger} M_{m}|\psi\rangle}} .
    $$

    The measurement operators satisfy the **completeness equation**,

    $$
    \sum_{m} M_{m}^{\dagger} M_{m}=I
    $$

!!! theorem "Theorem 2.2.14 : Completeness equation"
    The completeness equation in **Definition 2.2.13** is the condition that all outcome probabilities for a state add to one.

    $$
    1=\sum_{m} p(m)=\sum_{m}\langle\psi| M_{m}^{\dagger} M_{m}|\psi\rangle
    $$

    This equation being satisfied for all $|\psi\rangle$ is equivalent to the completeness equation.

    !!! proof
        Assume $\sum_{m} M_{m}^{\dagger} M_{m}=I$.
        Then, for every normalized state $|\psi\rangle$,
        
        $$
        \sum_{m} p(m)=\sum_{m}\langle\psi| M_{m}^{\dagger} M_{m}|\psi\rangle
        =\langle\psi|I|\psi\rangle
        =\langle\psi|\psi\rangle
        =1.
        $$
        
        Conversely, assume $\sum_{m}\langle\psi| M_{m}^{\dagger} M_{m}|\psi\rangle=1$ for every normalized $|\psi\rangle$.
        Let $A=\sum_{m} M_{m}^{\dagger} M_{m}$.
        Then $\langle\psi|A|\psi\rangle=1$ for every normalized $|\psi\rangle$.
        Hence $A=I$ by equality of quadratic forms, so $\sum_{m} M_{m}^{\dagger} M_{m}=I$.

!!! example "Example 2.2.15 : Measurement in the computational basis"
    A simple but important example of a measurement is the measurement of a qubit in the computational basis.
    This is a measurement on a single qubit with two outcomes defined by the two measurement operators $M_{0}=|0\rangle\langle 0|$ and $M_{1}=|1\rangle\langle 1|$.
    Observe that each measurement operator is Hermitian, and that $M_{0}^{2}=M_{0}$ and $M_{1}^{2}=M_{1}$.
    Thus the completeness relation is obeyed, according to **Theorem 2.2.14**, $I=M_{0}^{\dagger}M_{0}+M_{1}^{\dagger}M_{1}=M_{0}+M_{1}$.

    Suppose the state being measured is $|\psi\rangle=a|0\rangle+b|1\rangle$.
    Then the probability of obtaining measurement outcome $0$ is

    $$
    p(0)=\langle\psi|M_{0}^{\dagger}M_{0}|\psi\rangle=\langle\psi|M_{0}|\psi\rangle=|a|^{2}
    $$

    Similarly, the probability of obtaining the measurement outcome $1$ is $p(1)=|b|^{2}$.
    The state after measurement in the two cases is therefore

    $$
    \frac{M_{0}|\psi\rangle}{|a|}=\frac{a}{|a|}|0\rangle
    $$

    $$
    \frac{M_{1}|\psi\rangle}{|b|}=\frac{b}{|b|}|1\rangle.
    $$

    We will see in **Concept 2.2.31** that multipliers like $a /|a|$, which have modulus one, can effectively be ignored, so the two post-measurement states are effectively $|0\rangle$ and $|1\rangle$.

!!! theorem "Theorem 2.2.16 : Cascaded measurements are single measurements"
    Let $\{L_l\}$ and $\{M_m\}$ be two sets of measurement operators.
    A measurement defined by $\{L_l\}$ followed by a measurement defined by $\{M_m\}$ is physically equivalent to a single measurement with operators $\{N_{lm}\}$.

    If we define

    $$
    N_{lm} \equiv M_{m} L_{l}
    $$

    the equivalent single-measurement family is given by $\{N_{lm}\}$.

    !!! proof
        For a state $|\psi\rangle$, the two-step process gives outcome pair $(l,m)$ with probability

        $$
        p(l,m)=\langle\psi|L_l^\dagger M_m^\dagger M_m L_l|\psi\rangle.
        $$
        
        The corresponding normalized post-measurement state is

        $$
        \frac{M_m L_l|\psi\rangle}{\sqrt{p(l,m)}}.
        $$
        
        Using $N_{lm}=M_m L_l$, the single-measurement family gives

        $$
        p'(l,m)=\langle\psi|N_{lm}^\dagger N_{lm}|\psi\rangle
        =\langle\psi|L_l^\dagger M_m^\dagger M_m L_l|\psi\rangle
        =p(l,m),
        $$
        
        and post-measurement state

        $$
        \frac{N_{lm}|\psi\rangle}{\sqrt{p'(l,m)}}
        =\frac{M_mL_l|\psi\rangle}{\sqrt{p(l,m)}}.
        $$

        If $\sum_l L_l^\dagger L_l=I$ and $\sum_m M_m^\dagger M_m=I$, then

        $$
        \sum_{l,m} N_{lm}^\dagger N_{lm}
        =\sum_l L_l^\dagger\left(\sum_m M_m^\dagger M_m\right)L_l
        =\sum_l L_l^\dagger L_l
        =I.
        $$
        
        so $\{N_{lm}\}$ is a valid measurement.

## 2.2.4. Distinguishing Quantum States (application of postulate 3)

!!! concept "Concept 2.2.17 : Distinguishing quantum states"
    Distinguishing quantum states is introduced by the following game between two parties, Alice and Bob.
    Alice chooses a state $\left|\psi_{i}\right\rangle(1 \leq i \leq n)$ from a fixed set of states known to both parties.
    She sends the state $\left|\psi_{i}\right\rangle$ to Bob, whose task is to identify the index $i$ of the state she gave him.

!!! theorem "Theorem 2.2.18 : Orthonormal states are distinguishable"
    Suppose the states $\left\{\left|\psi_i\right\rangle\right\}$ are orthonormal.

    For each possible index $i\neq 0$, define the measurement operators

    $$
    M_i \equiv \left|\psi_i\right\rangle\left\langle\psi_i\right|.
    $$

    Define one additional operator

    $$
    M_0 \equiv \sqrt{I-\sum_{i \neq 0}\left|\psi_i\right\rangle\left\langle\psi_i\right|}.
    $$

    These operators satisfy the completeness relation.
    If the state $\left|\psi_i\right\rangle$ is prepared, then

    $$
    p(i)=\left\langle\psi_i\right|M_i\left|\psi_i\right\rangle=1.
    $$

    Therefore the result $i$ occurs with certainty.
    So it is possible to reliably distinguish the orthonormal states $\left|\psi_i\right\rangle$.

    !!! proof
        The proof is expanded by checking completeness first and then computing all relevant outcome probabilities.
        For each index $j$, the operator $\left|\psi_j\right\rangle\left\langle\psi_j\right|$ is positive semidefinite.
        Hence the finite sum

        $$
        S=\sum_{i \neq 0}\left|\psi_i\right\rangle\left\langle\psi_i\right|.
        $$

        is positive semidefinite.
        Orthogonality implies $\langle\phi|S|\phi\rangle \le \langle\phi|\phi\rangle$ for all vectors $|\phi\rangle$ in the span of the listed states.
        So the operator $I-S$ is positive semidefinite.
        Therefore its positive square root is well-defined, so $M_0$ is a valid measurement operator.
        Because $M_0^2=I-S$, we have

        $$
        M_0^\dagger M_0=I-\sum_{i \neq 0}\left|\psi_i\right\rangle\left\langle\psi_i\right|.
        $$

        Each projector $M_i$ is Hermitian and satisfies $M_i^\dagger=M_i$.
        Therefore

        $$
        \sum_{i \neq 0} M_i^\dagger M_i+M_0^\dagger M_0
        =\sum_{i \neq 0}\left|\psi_i\right\rangle\left\langle\psi_i\right|+I-\sum_{i \neq 0}\left|\psi_i\right\rangle\left\langle\psi_i\right|=I.
        $$

        So the set $\{M_i\}_{i \neq 0}\cup\{M_0\}$ is complete.

        Now assume the prepared state is $\left|\psi_k\right\rangle$ for some index $k$.
        For outcome $j\neq 0$, the probability is

        $$
        p(j|k)=\left\langle\psi_k\right|M_j\left|\psi_k\right\rangle
        =\left\langle\psi_k\middle|\psi_j\right\rangle\left\langle\psi_j\middle|\psi_k\right\rangle
        =\left|\left\langle\psi_j\middle|\psi_k\right\rangle\right|^2
        =\delta_{jk}.
        $$

        For $j=k$, this is one.
        For $j\neq k$, this is zero.
        For outcome $0$, we compute

        $$
        p(0|k)=\left\langle\psi_k\right|M_0^\dagger M_0\left|\psi_k\right\rangle
        =\left\langle\psi_k\right|\left(I-\sum_{i \neq 0}\left|\psi_i\right\rangle\left\langle\psi_i\right|\right)\left|\psi_k\right\rangle
        =1-\sum_{i \neq 0}\left|\left\langle\psi_i\middle|\psi_k\right\rangle\right|^2.
        $$

        If $k=0$, then all overlaps vanish and $p(0|0)=1$.
        If $k\neq 0$, the term $i=k$ in the sum is exactly one and all other overlaps vanish, giving $p(0|k)=0$.
        Thus each prepared state yields its own unique measurement outcome with unit probability.

!!! theorem "Theorem 2.2.19 : Non-orthogonal states cannot be reliably distinguished"
    If two candidate states are non-orthogonal, then no quantum measurement can distinguish them perfectly.
    Suppose Bob uses measurement operators $M_{j}$ and associates each outcome $j$ with a guess $i=f(j)$.
    If $f(j)=1$, then outcome $j$ is interpreted as $|\psi_{1}\rangle$.
    Because $|\psi_{2}\rangle$ has a component parallel to $|\psi_{1}\rangle$, this outcome can appear when $|\psi_{2}\rangle$ is prepared.
    So Bob can make an error.

    Any non-orthogonal pair $|\psi_{1}\rangle$ and $|\psi_{2}\rangle$ has a decomposition

    $$
    |\psi_{2}\rangle=\alpha|\psi_{1}\rangle+\beta|\varphi\rangle,
    $$

    where $|\varphi\rangle \perp |\psi_{1}\rangle$, $|\alpha|^{2}+|\beta|^{2}=1$, and $|\beta|<1$.

    !!! proof
        Assume, for contradiction, that a measurement can distinguish $|\psi_{1}\rangle$ and $|\psi_{2}\rangle$ reliably.
        Define

        $$
        E_{i}\equiv\sum_{j:f(j)=i}M_{j}^{\dagger}M_{j}.
        $$

        Perfect distinguishability gives

        $$
        \langle\psi_{1}|E_{1}|\psi_{1}\rangle=1 ,
        \quad
        \langle\psi_{2}|E_{2}|\psi_{2}\rangle=1.
        $$

        Because $\sum_{i}E_{i}=I$, we get

        $$
        \langle\psi_{1}|E_{2}|\psi_{1}\rangle=0,
        \quad
        \sqrt{E_{2}}|\psi_{1}\rangle=0.
        $$

        Substituting $|\psi_{2}\rangle=\alpha|\psi_{1}\rangle+\beta|\varphi\rangle$ gives

        $$
        \sqrt{E_{2}}|\psi_{2}\rangle=\beta\sqrt{E_{2}}|\varphi\rangle.
        $$

        so

        $$
        \langle\psi_{2}|E_{2}|\psi_{2}\rangle
        =|\beta|^{2}\langle\varphi|E_{2}|\varphi\rangle
        \le|\beta|^{2}<1.
        $$

        The second inequality follows from

        $$
        \langle\varphi|E_{2}|\varphi\rangle
        \le\sum_{i}\langle\varphi|E_{i}|\varphi\rangle
        =\langle\varphi|\varphi\rangle=1.
        $$

        This contradicts $\langle\psi_{2}|E_{2}|\psi_{2}\rangle=1$.
        Therefore no such perfect measurement exists.

## 2.2.5. Projective Measurements

!!! definition "Definition 2.2.20 : Projective measurements"
    A **projective measurement** is described by an observable $M$, a Hermitian operator on the state space of the system being observed.
    According to **Theorem 2.1.35**, the observable has a spectral decomposition.

    $$
    M=\sum_{m} m P_{m}
    $$

    where $P_{m}$ is the projector onto the eigenspace of $M$ with eigenvalue $m$.
    The possible outcomes of the measurement correspond to the eigenvalues $m$ of the observable.
    Given a state $|\psi\rangle$, the probability of obtaining outcome $m$ is

    $$
    p(m)=\langle\psi| P_{m}|\psi\rangle.
    $$

    Given that outcome $m$ occurred, the state of the quantum system immediately after measurement is

    $$
    \frac{P_{m}|\psi\rangle}{\sqrt{p(m)}}.
    $$

!!! theorem "Theorem 2.2.21 : Average value and standard deviation of an observable"
    The **average value** of the observable $M$ in state $|\psi\rangle$ is given by

    $$
    \mathbf{E}(M)=\sum_{m} m p(m)
    =\sum_{m} m\langle\psi| P_{m}|\psi\rangle
    =\langle\psi|(\sum_{m} m P_{m})|\psi\rangle
    =\langle\psi|M|\psi\rangle.
    $$

    We write this as $\langle M\rangle\equiv\langle\psi|M|\psi\rangle$.

    The square of the **standard deviation** is

    $$
    {[\Delta(M)]}^{2} =\left\langle(M-\langle M\rangle)^{2}\right\rangle
    =\left\langle M^{2}\right\rangle-\langle M\rangle^{2}.
    $$

    Hence repeated measurements of $M$ on state $|\psi\rangle$ have spread

    $$
    \Delta(M)=\sqrt{\left\langle M^{2}\right\rangle-\langle M\rangle^{2}}.
    $$

!!! concept "Concept 2.2.22 : Two projective-measurement nomenclatures"
    Two widely used nomenclatures for projective measurements deserve emphasis.
    
    Rather than giving an observable to describe a projective measurement, often people simply list a complete set of orthogonal projectors $P_{m}$ satisfying the relations

    $$
    \sum_{m} P_{m}=I, \quad P_{m}P_{m^{\prime}}=\delta_{m m^{\prime}}P_{m},
    $$

    according to **Definition 2.2.20**.
    The corresponding observable implicit in this usage is $M=\sum_{m} m P_{m}$.
    
    Another widely used phrase, to "measure in a basis $|m\rangle$", where $|m\rangle$ form an orthonormal basis, simply means to perform the projective measurement with projectors $P_{m}=|m\rangle\langle m|$.

!!! example "Example 2.2.23 : Measurement of $Z$"
    Let's look at an example of projective measurements on single qubits, the measurement of the observable $Z$.
    This has eigenvalues $+1$ and $-1$ with corresponding eigenvectors $|0\rangle$ and $|1\rangle$.
    Thus, for example, measurement of $Z$ on the state $|\psi\rangle=(|0\rangle+|1\rangle)/\sqrt{2}$ gives the result $+1$ with probability $\langle\psi \mid 0\rangle\langle 0 \mid \psi\rangle=1 / 2$.
    Similarly the result $-1$ occurs with probability $1 / 2$.

!!! example "Example 2.2.24 : Measurement of spin along the v axis"
    Let $\vec v=(v_{1},v_{2},v_{3})$ be any real three-dimensional unit vector.
    Define the observable

    $$
    \vec v \cdot\vec{\sigma}=v_{1}\sigma_{1}+v_{2}\sigma_{2}+v_{3}\sigma_{3}
    $$

    This is called a measurement of spin along the $\vec v$ axis.

    Because $\vec v$ is a unit vector, $(\vec v\cdot\vec{\sigma})^{2}=I$.
    Therefore, the eigenvalues of $\vec v\cdot\vec{\sigma}$ are $\pm1$.
    The projectors onto these eigenspaces are

    $$
    P_{\pm}=\frac{I\pm\vec v\cdot\vec{\sigma}}{2}
    $$

    For a measurement of $\vec v\cdot\vec{\sigma}$ on state $|0\rangle$, the probability of outcome $+1$ is

    $$
    p(+1)=\langle0|P_{+}|0\rangle=
    \frac{1+v_{3}}{2}.
    $$

    Conditional on obtaining $+1$, the post-measurement state is

    $$
    \frac{P_{+}|0\rangle}{\sqrt{p(+1)}}
    =\frac{(I+\vec v\cdot\vec{\sigma})|0\rangle}{\sqrt{2(1+v_{3})}}
    =\frac{(1+v_{3})|0\rangle+(v_{1}+i v_{2})|1\rangle}{\sqrt{2(1+v_{3})}}.
    $$

## 2.2.6. POVM Measurements

!!! concept "Concept 2.2.25 : POVM elements, POVM"
    In some applications only the outcome probabilities are relevant, since the post-measurement state is not needed.
    This is especially useful for a single measurement performed at the end of an experiment.

    Suppose the measurement has operators $M_{m}$ and the system is in state $|\psi\rangle$.
    The outcome probability is

    $$
    p(m)=\langle\psi| M_{m}^{\dagger} M_{m}|\psi\rangle.
    $$

    Define

    $$
    E_{m}\equiv M_{m}^{\dagger} M_{m}
    $$

    Then $E_{m}$ is a positive operator.
    By Postulate 3 (**Definition 2.2.13**), $\sum_{m}E_{m}=I$, which is the completeness condition of **Theorem 2.2.14**.

    The outcome probabilities can therefore be written as

    $$
    p(m)=\langle\psi| E_{m}|\psi\rangle.
    $$

    The set of operators $\{E_{m}\}$ is sufficient to determine the probabilities of all outcomes.
    The operators $E_{m}$ are the **POVM elements** associated with the measurement, and the set $\{E_{m}\}$ is called a **POVM**.

!!! example "Example 2.2.26 : POVM elements of the projective measurement"
    For an example of a POVM, consider a projective measurement described by measurement operators $P_m$.
    The projectors satisfy $P_m P_{m'}=\delta_{m m'} P_m$ and $\sum_m P_m=I$.
    In this instance, all POVM elements equal the measurement operators, since $E_m \equiv P_m^\dagger P_m = P_m$.

!!! theorem "Theorem 2.2.27 : POVM elements and measurement operators coincide iff projective measurement"
    A measurement is projective if and only if, for each outcome $m$, the POVM element equals the measurement operator.
    In this instance all POVM elements are the same as the measurement operators themselves, since

    $$
    E_m\equiv P_m^{\dagger}P_m=P_m.
    $$

    !!! proof
        - ($\Rightarrow$)  
            If the measurement is projective, the measurement operators are orthogonal projectors $P_m$, so $P_m=P_m^{\dagger}=P_m^2$.
            Therefore
            
            $$
            E_m\equiv P_m^{\dagger}P_m=P_m^2=P_m.
            $$
            
            Hence each POVM element is the same as its measurement operator.

        - ($\Leftarrow$)  
            If $E_m=M_m$ for each $m$, then by the POVM definition
            
            $$
            E_m=M_m^{\dagger}M_m=M_m,
            $$
            
            so $M_m$ is Hermitian and idempotent, namely $M_m^{\dagger}=M_m$ and $M_m^2=M_m$.
            A Hermitian idempotent operator is an orthogonal projector, so each measurement operator is a projector.
            Hence the measurement is projective.

!!! concept "Concept 2.2.28 : POVMs as positive complete operator sets"
    The POVM element associated with outcome $m$ is a positive operator $E_m$.
    For any state $|\psi\rangle$, outcome $m$ occurs with probability $p(m)=\langle\psi|E_m|\psi\rangle$.
    Suppose $\{E_m\}$ is an arbitrary set of positive operators with

    $$
    \sum_m E_m=I.
    $$

    Defining

    $$
    M_m\equiv\sqrt{E_m}
    $$

    gives

    $$
    \sum_m M_m^{\dagger}M_m=\sum_m E_m=I.
    $$

    Therefore $\{M_m\}$ describes a measurement with POVM $\{E_m\}$.
    A set $\{E_m\}$ is therefore called a **POVM** when
    
    - (a) each operator $E_m$ is positive.
    - (b) the completeness relation $\sum_m E_m=I$ holds, expressing that probabilities sum to one.

!!! example "Example 2.2.29 : Qubit distinguishing with certainty using a POVM"
    Suppose Alice gives Bob a qubit prepared in one of two states, $\left|\psi_{1}\right\rangle=|0\rangle$ or $\left|\psi_{2}\right\rangle=(|0\rangle+|1\rangle) / \sqrt{2}$.
    As explained in **Th**, it is impossible for Bob to determine whether he has been given $\left|\psi_{1}\right\rangle$ or $\left|\psi_{2}\right\rangle$ with perfect reliability.
    However, it is possible for him to perform a measurement that distinguishes the states some of the time and never misidentifies either state.
    Consider a POVM containing three elements

    $$
    E_{1} \equiv \frac{\sqrt{2}}{1+\sqrt{2}}|1\rangle\langle 1|
    $$

    $$
    E_{2} \equiv \frac{\sqrt{2}}{1+\sqrt{2}} \frac{(|0\rangle-|1\rangle)(\langle 0|-\langle 1|)}{2}
    $$

    $$
    E_{3} \equiv I-E_{1}-E_{2}
    $$

    It is straightforward to verify that these are positive operators and that they satisfy the completeness relation $\sum_{m} E_{m}=I$, so they form a legitimate POVM.
    
    Suppose Bob is given the state $\left|\psi_{1}\right\rangle=|0\rangle$ and performs the POVM $\left\{E_{1}, E_{2}, E_{3}\right\}$.
    There is zero probability that he observes outcome $E_{1}$, because $E_{1}$ has been chosen so that $\left\langle\psi_{1}\right| E_{1}\left|\psi_{1}\right\rangle=0$.
    Therefore, if his outcome is $E_{1}$, he can safely conclude the state was $\left|\psi_{2}\right\rangle$.
    A similar argument shows that an outcome $E_{2}$ means the state must have been $\left|\psi_{1}\right\rangle$.
    Sometimes Bob obtains outcome $E_{3}$ and in that case he can infer nothing about which state was sent.
    The key point is that Bob never makes a mistake identifying the state.
    This infallibility comes at the cost that Bob sometimes gains no information.
    The example shows how POVMs offer intuitive structure for quantum measurements when only outcome statistics matter.

!!! theorem "Theorem 2.2.30 : Unitary implementation of measurement operators"
    Suppose a measurement is described by measurement operators $\{M_m\}$, and let $E_m\equiv M_m^{\dagger} M_m$.
    Then there exist unitary operators $U_m$ such that $M_m=U_m\sqrt{E_m}$.

    !!! proof
        Apply the Polar Decomposition (**Theorem 2.1.72**) to the operator $A=M_m$.
        
        By polar decomposition, there exists a unitary operator $U_m$ and a positive operator $J_m$ such that
        
        $$
        M_m = U_m J_m,
        $$
        
        where
        
        $$
        J_m = \sqrt{M_m^\dagger M_m}.
        $$
        
        But by definition,
        
        $$
        E_m \equiv M_m^\dagger M_m.
        $$
        
        Therefore
        
        $$
        J_m = \sqrt{E_m}.
        $$
        
        Substituting this into the polar decomposition gives
        
        $$
        M_m = U_m \sqrt{E_m}.
        $$
        
        Hence for each measurement operator $M_m$, there exists a unitary operator $U_m$ such that
        
        $$
        M_m = U_m \sqrt{E_m}.
        $$
        
        This proves the claim.

In many instances later in the book we will only be concerned with measurement statistics, and will therefore use the POVM formalism rather than the more general formalism for measurements described by **Definition 2.2.13**.

## 2.2.7 Phase

!!! concept "Concept 2.2.31 : Global phase factor"
    We say the states $e^{i \theta}|\psi\rangle$ and $|\psi\rangle$ are the same up to the **global phase factor** $e^{i \theta}$.
    
    For a measurement operator $M_{m}$, the probability of outcome $m$ from $|\psi\rangle$ is $\langle\psi| M_{m}^{\dagger} M_{m}|\psi\rangle$.
    For $e^{i \theta}|\psi\rangle$, the corresponding probability is $\langle\psi| e^{-i\theta} M_{m}^{\dagger} M_{m} e^{i\theta}|\psi\rangle$.
    Since $e^{-i\theta}$ and $e^{i\theta}$ are scalar phases, this equals $\langle\psi| M_{m}^{\dagger} M_{m}|\psi\rangle$.
    Therefore these two states give identical measurement statistics, so global phase factors are physically irrelevant and can be ignored.

!!! concept "Concept 2.2.32 : Relative phase is basis-dependent"
    The two states

    $$
    \frac{|0\rangle+|1\rangle}{\sqrt{2}} \quad \text{and} \quad \frac{|0\rangle-|1\rangle}{\sqrt{2}}
    $$

    differ by a relative sign in the $|1\rangle$ amplitude.
    In both states, the magnitudes of the amplitudes are equal.
    More generally, two amplitudes $a$ and $b$ **differ by a relative phase** if there is a real $\theta$ such that $a=e^{i\theta}b$.
    Two states differ by a relative phase in a basis when each pair of corresponding amplitudes in that basis differs by such a phase factor.
    In the example above, the $|0\rangle$ amplitudes match with factor $1$, while the $|1\rangle$ amplitudes differ by a factor of $-1$.
    
    This is different from global phase, because relative phases can vary from amplitude to amplitude and therefore depend on the chosen basis.
    States related only by relative phases in some basis can therefore lead to different measurement statistics.
    They are not physically equivalent in the way global-phase-related states are.

## 2.2.8. Composite Systems

!!! definition "Definition 2.2.33 : Composite system state space"
    The **state space** of a composite physical system is the **tensor product** of the state spaces of the component physical systems.
    If systems are numbered $1$ through $n$, and system number $i$ is prepared in the state $|\psi_{i}\rangle$, then the joint state of the total system is

    $$
    |\psi_{1}\rangle \otimes|\psi_{2}\rangle \otimes\cdots\otimes|\psi_{n}\rangle.
    $$

!!! concept "Concept 2.2.34 : Subscript notation for local operators"
    At this point it suffices to mention a useful subscript notation to denote states and operators on different systems, when it is not clear from context.
    For example, in a system containing three qubits, $X_{2}$ is the Pauli $\sigma_{x}$ operator acting on the second qubit.

!!! theorem "Lemma 2.2.35 : Extension of an inner-product-preserving map"
    Suppose $V$ is a Hilbert space with subspace $W$.
    Suppose $U:W\rightarrow V$ is linear and satisfies, for all $|w_{1}\rangle$ and $|w_{2}\rangle$ in $W$,

    $$
    \langle w_{1}| U^{\dagger} U|w_{2}\rangle=\langle w_{1}| w_{2}\rangle
    $$

    Then there exists a unitary operator $U^{\prime}:V\rightarrow V$ such that $U^{\prime}|w\rangle=U|w\rangle$ for all $|w\rangle\in W$.
    Usually we omit the prime symbol and write $U$ for the extension.

    !!! proof
        Choose an orthonormal basis $\{|w_{i}\rangle\}_{i\in I}$ for $W$.
        Let $|u_{i}\rangle\equiv U|w_{i}\rangle$ for $i\in I$.
        The hypothesis gives $\langle u_{i}|u_{j}\rangle=\langle w_{i}|w_{j}\rangle$ for all $i,j\in I$.
        Hence $\{|u_{i}\rangle\}_{i\in I}$ is orthonormal.
        Extend this set to an orthonormal basis $\{|u_{i}\rangle\}_{i\in I}\cup\{|u_{j}^{\perp}\rangle\}_{j\in J}$ of $V$.
        Extend $\{|w_{i}\rangle\}_{i\in I}$ to an orthonormal basis $\{|w_{i}\rangle\}_{i\in I}\cup\{|w_{j}^{\perp}\rangle\}_{j\in J}$ of $V$.
        Define $U^{\prime}$ on basis vectors by $U^{\prime}|w_{i}\rangle=|u_{i}\rangle$ and $U^{\prime}|w_{j}^{\perp}\rangle=|u_{j}^{\perp}\rangle$.
        Since $U^{\prime}$ maps one orthonormal basis of $V$ to another, it is unitary.
        For any $|w\rangle\in W$, this gives $U^{\prime}|w\rangle=U|w\rangle$ by construction.
        So $U^{\prime}$ is the required unitary extension.

!!! concept "Concept 2.2.36 : Implementing general measurements from projective measurements with unitary dynamics"
    In **Theorem 2.2.30** we claimed that projective measurements together with unitary dynamics are sufficient to implement a general measurement.
    Suppose we have a quantum system with state space $Q$, and we want to perform a measurement described by measurement operators $M_{m}$ on the system $Q$.

    To model this, introduce an ancilla system with state space $M$, and choose an orthonormal basis $\{|m\rangle\}$ in one-to-one correspondence with measurement outcomes.
    Let $|0\rangle$ be a fixed state of $M$, and define $U$ on product states by

    $$
    U|\psi\rangle|0\rangle \equiv \sum_{m} M_{m}|\psi\rangle|m\rangle.
    $$

    Using the orthonormality of $\{|m\rangle\}$ and the completeness relation $\sum_{m} M_{m}^{\dagger} M_{m}=I$, we get

    $$
    \begin{align*}
    \langle\varphi|\langle 0| U^{\dagger} U|\psi\rangle|0\rangle
    &=\sum_{m, m^{\prime}}\langle\varphi| M_{m}^{\dagger} M_{m^{\prime}}|\psi\rangle\langle m|m^{\prime}\rangle \\
    &=\sum_{m}\langle\varphi| M_{m}^{\dagger} M_{m}|\psi\rangle \\
    &=\langle\varphi|\psi\rangle.
    \end{align*}
    $$

    By the results of **Lemma 2.2.35**, $U$ can be extended to a unitary operator on $Q\otimes M$, and we continue to denote it by $U$.
    Next, we measure the composite system with projectors $P_{m}\equiv I_{Q}\otimes|m\rangle\langle m|$.
    Outcome $m$ occurs with probability

    $$
    \begin{align*}
    p(m) &=\langle\psi|\langle 0| U^{\dagger} P_{m} U|\psi\rangle|0\rangle \\
    &=\sum_{m^{\prime},m^{\prime\prime}}\langle\psi| M_{m^{\prime}}^{\dagger}\langle m^{\prime}|\left(I_{Q}\otimes|m\rangle\langle m|\right) M_{m^{\prime\prime}}|\psi\rangle|m^{\prime\prime}\rangle \\
    &=\langle\psi| M_{m}^{\dagger} M_{m}|\psi\rangle.
    \end{align*}
    $$

    The post-measurement joint state conditioned on $m$ is

    $$
    \frac{P_{m} U|\psi\rangle|0\rangle}{\sqrt{\langle\psi| U^{\dagger} P_{m} U|\psi\rangle}}=
    \frac{M_{m}|\psi\rangle|m\rangle}{\sqrt{\langle\psi| M_{m}^{\dagger} M_{m}|\psi\rangle}}.
    $$

    This gives ancilla state $|m\rangle$ and system state

    $$
    \frac{M_{m}|\psi\rangle}{\sqrt{\langle\psi| M_{m}^{\dagger} M_{m}|\psi\rangle}}.
    $$

    Therefore, unitary dynamics, projective measurements, and ancillas together realize any measurement of the form specified in Postulate 3.

!!! definition "Definition 2.2.37 : Entangled state"
    A state of a composite system is called an **entangled state** if it cannot be written as a product of states of its component systems.
    
    For reasons which nobody fully understands, entangled states play a crucial role in quantum computation and quantum information.
    They arise repeatedly throughout the rest of this book.

!!! theorem "Theorem 2.2.38 : $\frac{|00\rangle+|11\rangle}{\sqrt{2}}$ is entangled"
    The two-qubit state

    $$
    |\psi\rangle=\frac{|00\rangle+|11\rangle}{\sqrt{2}}
    $$

    cannot be written as a tensor product of single-qubit states.

    !!! proof
        Suppose $|a\rangle$ and $|b\rangle$ are single qubit states such that $|\psi\rangle=|a\rangle|b\rangle$.
        Write $|a\rangle=a_0|0\rangle+a_1|1\rangle$ and $|b\rangle=b_0|0\rangle+b_1|1\rangle$.
        Then

        $$
        |a\rangle|b\rangle=a_0b_0|00\rangle+a_0b_1|01\rangle+a_1b_0|10\rangle+a_1b_1|11\rangle.
        $$

        Matching coefficients with $|\psi\rangle$ gives $a_0b_0=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}$, $a_0b_1=0$, $a_1b_0=0$, and $a_1b_1=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}$.
        From $a_1b_1=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}$, we get $a_1\neq0$ and $b_1\neq0$.
        Then $a_1b_0=0$ implies $b_0=0$.
        But then $a_0b_0=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}$ cannot hold.
        Therefore no such pair $|a\rangle$, $|b\rangle$ exists.
